What a septic inspection is in North Carolina

When a home with a septic system changes hands, the tank and drain field are the one part of the property nobody can see, and in the foothills and mountains they're often the part that decides whether the deal closes on schedule. A home inspection doesn't cover them. The NC Home Inspector Licensure Board's Standards of Practice don't require a home inspector to inspect on-site waste disposal systems, so the septic inspection is a separate job with its own rules and its own certification.

Those rules come from the NC On-Site Wastewater Contractors and Inspectors Certification Board. Under N.C.G.S. 90A-72, anyone who conducts or offers a septic inspection connected to a sale, refinance or transfer of title has to be certified by that board, and the board's rules in 21 NCAC 39 spell out the minimum a point-of-sale inspection has to cover. I'm NC On-Site Wastewater Inspector #6007I, and that minimum is the floor for every inspection I do, not the ceiling.

What gets checked

The state's minimum point-of-sale inspection (21 NCAC 39 .1006) starts before anyone touches a shovel, with two numbers that have to match up: how many bedrooms the house is advertised with, and how many bedrooms the system was designed for according to the county's permit. From there, the inspection covers the system component by component.

Records and setup

  • Advertised bedroom count versus the designed system size on the county operation or repair permit
  • Type of water supply (public or private well)
  • Whether the system requires a certified operator, and recent operator reports if it does
  • Anything that limited or prevented part of the inspection

Septic tank

  • Location and distances, access risers, lid condition
  • Inlet and outlet baffles or tees, and the effluent filter if there is one
  • Water level, signs of leakage, inflow when water is run from the house
  • Roots, pipe connections, the date it was last pumped, and the solids level

Pump tank, if the system has one

  • Lids and distances, the location and condition of the control panel
  • Whether the alarm works and the pump runs
  • Anything, like power being off, that kept the pump system from being fully evaluated

Drain field (dispersal field)

  • Type of field, distances, number and length of lines
  • Surfacing effluent, traffic over the field, vegetation and drainage as they affect the system
  • Whether effluent is actually reaching the field

The rules also say what an inspector has to do, not just look at: uncover the tank lids and distribution device unless something blocks it, probe components that look deteriorated, open anything readily accessible, and report any sign of water getting in or out where it shouldn't. That's why a septic inspection involves digging and why it takes longer than people expect.

How the day goes

1. Find the system

If the county has a permit or layout drawing on file, it's the starting point. Anything the owner knows helps. Then I locate the tank on site and mark where it sits.

2. Uncover and open the tank

Most tanks in this area have buried lids. I dig them up by hand when it's practical and with a mini excavator when it isn't, then the lid comes off.

If you're the buyer: the seller needs to know in advance that the inspection involves digging in the yard. Your agent can handle that when the inspection is scheduled.

3. Evaluate the tank and its parts

With the lid off I evaluate the tank walls and lid, the inlet and outlet tees, the effluent filter if there is one, the water level, roots, and whether water is moving through the way it should when it's run from the house.

4. Check the drain field

Most of the drain field is underground, so this part is about what the ground tells you: wet or spongy spots, surfacing effluent, odors, settling, vehicles driving over the lines, drainage running toward the field, and whether liquid leaving the tank is getting there at all.

5. Report

You get a written report with photos that says what I found and what it means for the next step. The rules allow ten business days; I don't use them.

Pumping at the inspection

The state's inspection rule assumes the tank may be pumped during the inspection, and it's the best way to see the tank itself: the walls, the floor, the baffles and the condition of the concrete only show once the liquid is out. It's also when a pumping company can tell you how much sludge had built up. I don't pump, but I can arrange a pumping company for the same visit so it all happens in one trip. If you'd rather not pump, that's your call; the report will carry the statement the rules require when a client declines pumping. How pumping with the inspection works.

What the report says, and what it doesn't

A point-of-sale septic inspection is an evaluation of the visible condition of the system on the day of the inspection. It isn't a pass/fail certificate, and the rules are specific about what it doesn't include: how long a component will last, what caused a problem, how to fix it or what it will cost, whether the property suits a particular use, or its market value (21 NCAC 39 .1004). When something warrants it, the report refers you to the county health department or a certified contractor, which is exactly what the rules call for and exactly why it helps that I'm neither.

Repairs to a septic system are generally done under a repair permit from the county health department by a certified contractor. I don't do that work, so nothing in my report is a bid.

Owners: refinance, maintenance and after a flood

Not every inspection is tied to a sale. Lenders sometimes ask for one on a refinance. NCDHHS recommends having a system inspected about every three years and pumping as needed, typically every three to five years, which is cheap insurance on a system that costs real money to replace. And after Hurricane Helene, NCDHHS advised having flooded systems inspected, with soil settling over the tank or field, parts that moved or surfaced, and sewage backing up as the warning signs to watch for. If a property in Burke, McDowell, Caldwell or Buncombe County sat in water in 2024, the septic system deserves a look.

Schedule it with the home inspection

Septic inspection FAQ

How long does a septic inspection take?

Plan on a couple of hours on site, longer if the tank is deep, on a steep lot, or hard to find. Pumping at the same visit adds the pumping company's time. The written report follows after the inspection.

How far ahead should I book?

As soon as the contract is signed. The digging, a pumping company if you want one, and any county records all take coordination, and your due diligence period doesn't pause for them. Call or book online with the address and your deadline and I'll work backward from it.

Can I be there?

Yes. Buyers and agents are welcome, and the end of the inspection is a good time to see the open tank and ask questions. If you can't make it, the photos in the report show the same things.

What if the tank can't be found?

Then finding it is the first job. The county permit or layout drawing, if one exists, narrows it down, and so does anything the owner remembers. Some older systems have no permit at all; Buncombe County, for example, didn't issue septic permits before 1977. The report states whatever limited the inspection, as the rules require.

Do you put the yard back?

The hole over the lid is filled back in when I'm done. It's still a dug spot in the lawn for a while, so sellers should expect that.

Does the inspection include the well?

No. The septic inspection records what type of water supply the property has, but well water testing is a separate service. It can be added to the same visit through Inspect-All Home Inspections.

What's the difference between this and a permit or "existing system verification"?

A county permit shows what was approved, sometimes decades ago. Some counties also offer an existing-system check for adding a bedroom or a structure. Neither one opens the tank and evaluates its condition today. That's what the point-of-sale inspection does.

Is it a pass or fail?

No. It's an evaluation of the visible condition of the system that day, with findings and referrals where they're warranted. Lenders, buyers and sellers then decide what to do with that information.
