Why pump during the inspection, not before

People sometimes have the tank pumped a few days before the inspection to "get it ready." It's the wrong order. The water level in the tank and the way water moves through it when it's run from the house are evidence. A tank that's running high, or one where the inlet is backing up, tells you something about the drain field, and pumping erases that until the tank refills. The state's point-of-sale inspection rule (21 NCAC 39 .1006) lists the water level, inflow and the solids level among the items to evaluate, and it specifically contemplates the tank being pumped at the inspection, with a required statement in the report if the client declines.

So the sequence that works is: I open the tank and check the level, the tees and the flow first. Then the pumping company empties it. Then I evaluate what the liquid was hiding: the walls, the floor, the baffles, the condition of the concrete, and any cracks or holes.

How it works

  1. Book the inspection and check the box for pumping, or just tell me on the phone.
  2. I schedule a pumping company to meet me at the property at the inspection time. Pumping in North Carolina is done by companies permitted through the state's septage management program, and I work with ones that show up when they say they will.
  3. Inspection first, then pumping, then the empty-tank evaluation, in that order, all in the same visit.
  4. The pumping company bills you directly at its own rate. My scheduling fee for arranging it is quoted up front when you book and listed on the inspection agreement. I don't accept anything from the pumping company for the referral, which is how the board's rules for inspectors read and how I'd want it done if I were the client.

Pumping FAQ

Do I have to pump the tank for the inspection?

No. It's your choice. The inspection can be done without pumping; the report will then carry the statement the state rules require when a client declines to have the tank pumped at inspection. Pumping gives a better look at the tank itself.

Who pays the pumping company?

You do, directly, at the pumping company's rate. I don't mark up their work or take a referral fee from them. My scheduling fee is separate, quoted when you book, and shown on the agreement.

Should the seller pump the tank before listing?

Not before an inspection. Pump it at the inspection, after the water level and flow have been evaluated. If the seller wants to pump as part of a pre-listing inspection, the same sequence applies and the pumping receipt becomes part of the disclosure package.

Can pumping hide a problem?

Pumping a day or two before the inspection can. The inspector then sees a near-empty tank and has no way to judge the water level or whether the field is accepting effluent. That's why the order matters: inspect, then pump, then evaluate the empty tank.

What if the pumping company finds something?

Then it goes in my report, with photos, along with anything else I find. If the condition warrants it, the report refers you to the county health department or a certified contractor. I don't do repairs, so the referral is the end of my involvement unless you want a follow-up inspection later.
