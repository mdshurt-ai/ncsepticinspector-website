The three disclosure questions every septic seller answers

North Carolina's Residential Property and Owners' Association Disclosure Statement asks sellers three things about the septic system: what kind of sewage system the home has, whether you know how many bedrooms the septic permit allows, and whether there is any problem, malfunction or defect with the system. Most sellers answer the last two with "no representation" because they've never opened the tank and have no idea what the permit says. That's allowed. It's also where deals go sideways later, because the buyer's inspector will open the tank, and the buyer's agent will pull the permit.

A pre-listing inspection lets you answer from knowledge instead of hope. You'll know the permitted bedroom count before the listing goes live, and you'll know the condition of the tank, the tees, the drain field area and the pump system if there is one, with photos.

Why the bedroom count matters more than people think

North Carolina septic permits are issued for a number of bedrooms, because bedrooms are how the state estimates the daily wastewater flow the system has to handle. If a house is advertised as four bedrooms and the permit says three, that's not a detail; it's the first thing the state's point-of-sale inspection rule tells an inspector to compare (21 NCAC 39 .1006). Finished basements, bonus rooms and additions are the usual reasons the two numbers drift apart. Finding out at the listing stage means you can market the house accurately, or talk to the county about what it would take to change the permit, before a buyer's lender gets involved.

What the inspection includes

The same point-of-sale inspection a buyer would order, done for you: locate and mark the tank, uncover and open it, evaluate the tank and its components, check the drain field area, and put it all in a written report with photos. The full process is on the septic inspection page. If you want the tank pumped, I'll arrange a pumping company for the same visit, and the pumping receipt goes in your disclosure package next to the report.

What you do with the results

  • Nothing wrong? The report, the permit and a pumping receipt become part of your disclosure package. Buyers may still order their own inspection, and many do, but they walk in knowing what to expect instead of looking for a reason to renegotiate.
  • Something needs attention? You deal with it on your schedule, with the contractor you choose, under a county repair permit where one is required. That's a very different position from fixing it in the last week of a due diligence period with a buyer holding the deadline.
  • Permit doesn't match the house? You find out now, while there's time to talk to the county's environmental health office about options, and you avoid advertising a bedroom count the permit doesn't support.
What a pre-listing inspection isn't. It isn't a certification, a warranty or a guarantee that the buyer's inspector will agree with every line. It's an evaluation of the visible condition of the system on the day I'm there, by a state-certified inspector who doesn't pump or repair and therefore has no reason to find more or less than what's there.

Timing and what to expect in the yard

Do it before the listing photos. Uncovering a buried tank means a hole in the lawn, and while it's filled back in when I'm done, it's a visible patch for a few weeks. Scheduling it a couple of weeks before the photographer comes avoids a dirt rectangle in your listing pictures.

Plan on a couple of hours on site, longer if the tank is deep or hard to locate. Have the yard accessible, gates unlocked and dogs put up, and if you know where the tank is, mark it or tell me.

Pre-listing FAQ

Will the buyer accept my inspection instead of ordering their own?

Some will, some won't, and the buyer's lender may have its own requirement. Either way, you've already seen what their inspector will see, which is the point. A recent report with photos and a pumping receipt changes the tone of the inspection conversation.

Do I have to disclose what the inspection finds?

North Carolina's disclosure statement asks whether there is any problem, malfunction or defect with the septic system, and you answer it under the rules of the form. Talk to your agent or attorney about how to handle a specific finding. What a pre-listing inspection changes is that you answer from knowledge, with the option to fix things first.

Should I pump the tank before listing?

Pump it at the inspection, not before. The water level and flow in the tank are part of the evaluation, and pumping ahead of time erases them until the tank refills. I can arrange pumping for the same visit.

What if nobody knows where the tank is?

That happens a lot with older homes. The county permit or layout drawing narrows the search when one exists, and locating the tank is part of the inspection. Once it's found and marked, you'll want to keep that information for the buyer; it saves them digging through the yard looking for it.
