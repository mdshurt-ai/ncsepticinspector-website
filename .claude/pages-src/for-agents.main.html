How to order

Book online, call or text, or send the request form. Here's what moves the appointment fastest:

  • Property address and the client's name and phone
  • The due diligence or closing date
  • How many bedrooms the home is advertised with
  • Whether the client wants the tank pumped at the inspection
  • Whether anyone knows where the tank is, and whether a permit is on file
  • Gate codes, dogs, and whether the seller has been told the yard will be dug

The seller needs to know in advance that uncovering the tank means digging. Listing agents usually handle that in one sentence, and it saves a stopped inspection.

What your client gets

A point-of-sale inspection that follows the NC On-Site Wastewater Contractors and Inspectors Certification Board rules for what gets checked and what the report has to say (21 NCAC 39 .1006): the advertised bedroom count compared with the permit, the tank, the pump system if there is one, the drain field, and anything that limited the inspection. Findings come with photos and, where the condition warrants it, a referral to the county health department or a certified contractor. The rules allow ten business days for the written report; your client's deadline is what I actually work to.

I don't pump, install or repair septic systems, which means the report is never the first page of an estimate. That's the version of "independent" that holds up when a buyer's attorney reads it.

For home inspectors

Under the NC Home Inspector Licensure Board's Standards of Practice, you're not required to inspect on-site waste disposal systems, and most inspectors don't. When your client needs the septic done, I handle the septic and nothing else. The report goes to your client, your client stays your client, and I'm not going to pitch them on anything beyond the tank in the ground. If it's helpful, I'll coordinate my visit with yours so the buyer makes one trip.

I'm a licensed home inspector myself, so I know what you need from the septic side: a clear scope line between your report and mine, no overlap, and no surprises for the client about who's responsible for what.

For attorneys and lenders

North Carolina requires anyone who conducts or offers a septic inspection in connection with a sale, refinance or transfer of title to be certified by the NC On-Site Wastewater Contractors and Inspectors Certification Board (N.C.G.S. 90A-72). I'm Inspector #6007I. The report covers the items the board's minimum point-of-sale inspection rule requires, states anything that prevented or hindered part of the inspection, and, when a client declines pumping at the inspection, carries the statement and signature line the rule calls for. If your file needs a specific form or wording, send it with the order.

The rules we both work under

Two sets of rules shape how I work with agents, and they're worth saying out loud. As a licensed NC home inspector, I can't compensate anyone for referrals or for a spot on a list of inspectors, and I don't (11 NCAC 08 .1116). As a certified on-site wastewater inspector, I can't accept or offer commissions or allowances from anyone else involved in the job (21 NCAC 39 .0801). So there's nothing in it for you except a report that holds up and an inspector who answers the phone. If your clients come back, that's the whole arrangement.

Scheduling notes that save headaches

  • Pumping: if the client wants it, I schedule a pumping company for the inspection time. The pumping company bills the client directly; my scheduling fee is on the agreement. Don't have the seller pump ahead of time; the water level is evidence. Why the order matters.
  • Records: the county permit is the fastest way to find the tank and the only way to settle the bedroom count. Where to get it, county by county.
  • Site conditions: steep lots, wooded lots, deep tanks and frozen ground all add time. Say so when you book and I'll block enough of the day.
  • Bundling: home inspection, radon and well water testing can be scheduled for the same visit through my other company, Inspect-All Home Inspections.

Agent FAQ

Who gets the report?

The client who ordered the inspection, and you when the client has you on the order. Reports are delivered electronically with photos.

Can the buyer attend?

Yes, and the end of the inspection is the useful part to attend: the tank is open and the findings are in front of them. If they can't, the photos show the same things.

What if the seller refuses to allow digging?

Then the tank can't be opened and the inspection can't meet the state's minimum. I'll document the limitation as the rules require, but a septic inspection without access to the tank isn't one I'd want my client relying on. It's worth settling access before the inspection day.

How quickly can you get out there?

Call with the deadline and I'll tell you honestly. Pumping companies and county records add lead time, so the earlier in the due diligence period the better.

Do you give repair estimates?

No. The state rules exclude causes, methods and costs of corrections from the inspection, and I don't do repair work. The report refers the client to the county health department or a certified contractor when a condition warrants it.
